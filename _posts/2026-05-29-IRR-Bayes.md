It's time to stop simply acknowledging and ignoring data uncertainty, thinking that there is nothing we can do about it.

# Introduction

No matter how complex the models or data are, no matter how much data we collect, there will always be uncertainty. We cannot afford to ignore
this uncertainty, but what can we do if not acknowledge and ignore? Well, thankfully there is a cute little approach that was developed centuries
ago that easily allow us to not only measure, but also appreciate and incorporate data uncertainty; namely *Bayesian*.

Now, *Bayesian* statistics started out as a grandiose idea, but with too little time and power to make the actual calculations. However, there
there is no reason to opt for a simple, and confusing frequentist approach today when even the oldest smartphone has enough power to make a 
Bayesian interpretation. I will not go into the advantages of Bayesian statistics or how these are calculated within this post, but I will use
a Bayesian framework to outline the power this has for taking data uncertainty and converting it into meaningful interpretations. In particular,
I focus on an archaeological dataset to emphasise just how different the data interpretations become when we incorporate known errors.

# Background

I choose archaeological data because I had spent a long time in this domain and I know just how messy and uncertain their data are. I further 
extract measured uncertainty via an inter-rater reliability (IRR) project undergone by Pargeter and Colleagues. These IRR project aim to measure 
individual and group variation in stone tool attributes. The larger the variation, the more disagreement between individuals or groups, with the 
goal being to make the measurements as standard as possible so that archaeological interpretations can be comparable across any site in the 
world and through time. However, this study is one of the few approaches to do so. Most other studies argue that uncertainty should simply be 
acknowledged but ultimately ignored in favor of fostering a more inclusive environment for research groups to define their own definitions and
recording protocols.

Ultimately, the current research proposes either 1) standardize measurements to reduce variability and increase comparability or, 2) ignore this
uncertainty and move forward knowing it will always exist. I say, why not do both? I am here to claim and show that Bayesian statistical models 
can provide a means of doing both, offering a solution to this debated problem.

# Goals

The goal of the study was to evaluate whether I could reasonably incorporate measured unccertainty in artifact recording to standardize data 
interpretations. In particular, I aimed to 1) apply uncertainty from IRR studies to empirical archaeological assemblages and 2) evaluate whether 
the revised data showed similar or statistically different conclusions. In the end, I show that archaeologists should be very concerned. I show 
that, not only can I easily apply these data into regression analysese, but the resulting archaeological interpretations become largely different
based on who records the lithic assemblages. This implies that, by not accounting for this variability earlier, archaeological interpretations are
most definitely not standard acrosss sites or time periods and the interpretation is direclty dependent on the researcher, and not the empirical
data.
