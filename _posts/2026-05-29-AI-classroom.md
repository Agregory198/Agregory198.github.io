title: "AI in the Classroom: Learning Avoidance or Advantage?"

The Artificial Intelligence arms race is here, and education is at its center. The technology has sent tremors across the Earth as educators and students struggle to understand its role in their workplace and daily life. For many, AI is a one-stop shop to quench an unyielding thirst for knowledge, or in some cases, an unyielding laziness.

Currently, you can find AI anywhere with a connection to the internet, which at this time includes outer space; it's free, it’s powerful, it’s all-knowing. After all, humans designed AI software to learn from human knowledge, so of course it thinks it knows everything.

I was reluctant at first, repeatedly annoying my colleagues with comments like, “I know how to research, I know how to google, I know how to read, write, and speak; why do I need software to do it for me?” Now, I use some form of AI daily, for anything ranging from a light-hearted chat about my day to removing nonsense from my writing, or, for my unique passion, to learn more about statistical models. I actually think it has been my privilege to slowly adopt AI and realize just how powerful the software is for communicating and learning, when used correctly.

With the widespread use of AI, some universities across the world have banned all AI use by students and faculty. But, with the freely accessible software at everyone’s fingertips, bans on AI are hardly enforceable. Other scholars and campuses have expressed interest in integrating AI into their curriculum.

The lack of consensus has put academia between a rock and a beautiful place. At one end, educators and students do nothing and remain AI-illiterate, leaving them naïve and ignorant of novel tools and practices. On the other end, we step out of hiding, adopt AI-literacy, and our educators, students, and children learn how to use AI ethically and critically, preparing them, and us, for the world ahead.
In the absence of regulated AI-use, how do faculty and scholars ethically adopt these tools in our classrooms and professional careers?
In 2024, a study on undergraduate students from a U.S. university examined how AI facilitated not only completion of homework assignments but also learning outcomes. The majority of students said that they not only favored policies allowing AI use, but they also felt that they learned more from AI than they would have without it. University policies regulating how and when AI is used can benefit all students, the researchers concluded.

Another study, conducted in 2025 at a Swiss university, explored the challenges and advantages of AI regulation. This university had fully accepted the use of AI as soon as it became widely available. However, university leaders quickly realized that faculty and students were “doing it wrong.” Students and faculty were not familiar with how to interact with AI and were, more often than not, afraid to use it.

To account for this lack of knowledge and fear, the Swiss university has begun to restructure their entire curriculum to staff and mandate classes for faculty and students on how to use and when to use AI. 

More campuses should consider the latter approach. By opening positions for trained specialists in AI, universities can gain confidence that faculty and students stay up-to-date with current trends and have the knowledge to use AI safely and ethically inside and outside the classroom.

AI is here and it isn’t going anywhere. We know it benefits students’ learning, and we know there are measures we can take to regulate the knowledge of AI, how and when students should use it. Therefore, instead of remaining ignorant about the software, adopt it, use it, advance your life, and your world.

AI can be our ally; we don’t want to see a world where we make it our enemy.

Alex Gregory is a PhD candidate at the department of Anthropology at New York University and a member of the African Paleosciences Laboratory.
